<p>
	Search for the best hospital management software in India and you will find lists of twenty
	products, each one "the best". None of them knows your hospital. A 40-bed multispecialty hospital
	in Patna and a 400-bed tertiary centre in Mumbai do not need the same system, and the one that
	wins a list may be the one that fails your billing counter on a busy Monday.
</p>
<p>
	This guide is for the owner, director or medical superintendent of a hospital or nursing home of
	roughly 20 to 100 beds. It says what the software must cover, what to ask in a demo, and how to
	switch without putting a single patient at risk.
</p>

<h2 id="fit">Start with your hospital, not the product list</h2>
<p>Before you look at any product, write down four things. The shortlist follows from them.</p>
<ul>
	<li>
		<strong>Beds and departments.</strong> How many beds, which specialties, whether you run an OT,
		an ICU, dialysis or a diagnostic wing.
	</li>
	<li>
		<strong>How patients pay.</strong> The share of cash, insurance through a TPA, PMJAY or a state
		scheme, and corporate credit. Billing is where most hospital software fails.
	</li>
	<li>
		<strong>Who will use it.</strong> The front desk, nurses on the floor, the pharmacist, the lab,
		the billing team, and you. Each needs a screen that fits their job, not one screen for everyone.
	</li>
	<li>
		<strong>What you run today.</strong> Paper, spreadsheets, an old HMS, or a mix. This decides how
		the switch has to be planned.
	</li>
</ul>

<h2 id="modules">What a hospital management system must cover</h2>
<p>For a small or mid-sized multispecialty hospital, these are the parts that matter every day.</p>
<ul>
	<li>
		<strong>Registration and appointments</strong>: one patient record from the first visit,
		doctors' schedules, the OPD queue.
	</li>
	<li>
		<strong>OPD consultations</strong>: vitals, the doctor's notes, diagnoses, prescriptions and
		orders for the lab or pharmacy, without a second entry.
	</li>
	<li>
		<strong>Admission, the bed board and discharge</strong>: who is in which bed, transfers, the
		running bill, and a discharge summary that does not take an hour.
	</li>
	<li>
		<strong>OT</strong>: the schedule, the surgeon and team, consumables charged to the right bill.
	</li>
	<li>
		<strong>Pharmacy</strong>: stock by batch and expiry, issues to wards charged to the patient,
		GST on every sale.
	</li>
	<li>
		<strong>Lab</strong>: orders from OPD and wards, results back to the record and the bill.
	</li>
	<li>
		<strong>Billing</strong>: packages, deposits and refunds, discounts with an approver, TPA
		pre-authorisation and claims, room-rent limits, and government schemes.
	</li>
	<li>
		<strong>The owner's view</strong>: today's collections, occupancy, pending claims and dues, on
		one screen, every morning.
	</li>
</ul>

<h2 id="pressures">Why hospitals are looking now</h2>
<p>
	Most hospitals that read a guide like this already run some software. What makes them look is
	rarely a nicer screen. It is a pressure the old system was not built for.
</p>
<ul>
	<li>
		<strong>Patient data rules.</strong> The Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 treats a
		patient's records as personal data that the hospital must protect and explain. See
		<a href="/in/blog/dpdp-for-clinics/">what DPDP means for a clinic or hospital</a>.
	</li>
	<li>
		<strong>ABDM.</strong> Facility registration on HFR, doctors on HPR and ABHA at the front desk
		are increasingly asked for. Our
		<a href="/in/blog/abdm-abha-guide/">ABDM and ABHA guide</a> sets out the pieces.
	</li>
	<li>
		<strong>Billing that leaks.</strong> Unbilled consumables, short-paid claims and discounts
		nobody approved are the money a hospital loses without seeing it.
	</li>
	<li>
		<strong>Support that does not answer.</strong> A system nobody updates or supports is a risk in
		a hospital that never closes.
	</li>
</ul>

<h2 id="questions">The questions to ask in every demo</h2>
<p>
	A demo shows the vendor's best path. These questions make it show yours. Bring last month's bills
	and ask the vendor to run three of them through the system in front of you.
</p>
<ol>
	<li>
		<strong>
			Admit a patient, move them to another bed, add an OT and pharmacy charges, and discharge them.
		</strong>{" "}
		Does the bill come out right without anyone typing a charge twice?
	</li>
	<li>
		<strong>Show me a TPA case from pre-authorisation to settlement</strong>, including a short
		payment.
	</li>
	<li>
		<strong>Show me a package.</strong> What happens when the patient stays two days longer than the
		package allows?
	</li>
	<li>
		<strong>Who can give a discount, and where is it recorded?</strong> An approver and a reason on
		every discount is the cheapest control a hospital can have.
	</li>
	<li>
		<strong>Show me the pharmacy's stock by batch and expiry</strong>, and a ward issue charged to
		the patient.
	</li>
	<li>
		<strong>What do I see at 8 in the morning?</strong> Collections, occupancy, pending claims.
	</li>
	<li>
		<strong>How does my data come out if I leave?</strong> In what format, how fast, and at what
		cost.
	</li>
	<li>
		<strong>How do we move from our current system?</strong> Who moves the data, what does it cost,
		and does the old system stay on while we check?
	</li>
	<li>
		<strong>Who answers when the billing counter is stuck at 10 at night?</strong> A name and a
		number, not a ticket portal.
	</li>
	<li>
		<strong>What does it cost for my bed count, per year</strong>, including implementation and
		training?
	</li>
</ol>

<h2 id="switch">How to switch without risking a single patient</h2>
<p>
	The reason most hospitals stay on software they dislike is fear of the switch: a night of downtime
	in a hospital that never closes. That fear is right about a single cutover and wrong about a
	careful one.
</p>
<ul>
	<li>
		<strong>Run both, one department at a time.</strong> Start with the front desk or the pharmacy,
		keep the old system on, and compare the day's figures until they agree.
	</li>
	<li>
		<strong>Move the data before you move the people.</strong> Patients, doctors, the tariff and the
		pharmacy stock come across first, and are checked against the old system.
	</li>
	<li>
		<strong>Train on your own cases.</strong> Staff learn faster on last week's patients than on a
		vendor's demo hospital.
	</li>
	<li>
		<strong>Retire the old system last</strong>, when nobody has opened it for a week.
	</li>
</ul>

<h2 id="faq">Questions hospital owners ask</h2>
<p>
	<strong>What is the best hospital management software in India?</strong> The one that runs your
	own hospital's day without a spreadsheet beside it. For a 20 to 100 bed multispecialty hospital
	that means registration, OPD, admissions and the bed board, OT, pharmacy, lab, and billing that
	handles packages, TPA and PMJAY in one system, with your data exportable whenever you ask.
	Shortlist two or three products and test them on your own last month of bills.
</p>
<p>
	<strong>What modules does a hospital management system need?</strong> For a small or mid-sized
	hospital: registration and appointments, OPD consultations, admission and discharge with a bed
	board, OT scheduling, pharmacy with batches and expiry, lab orders and reports, billing with
	packages, deposits, TPA and government schemes, and the owner's daily view of collections and
	occupancy. Payroll and accounts can sit in the same system or beside it.
</p>
<p>
	<strong>How much does hospital management software cost in India?</strong> Hospital pricing is
	usually quoted per bed or per user, with a separate implementation fee. Ask for the yearly figure
	for your bed count, what the implementation and data migration cost, and what you pay to get your
	data out if you leave. Lucoze prices hospitals by bed count and charges nothing for migrating your
	data.
</p>
<p>
	<strong>How long does it take to switch hospital software?</strong> Weeks, not a weekend. The safe
	way is to run the new system beside the old one, department by department, until the figures
	agree, and only then retire the old one. A vendor who proposes a single cutover night for a
	24-hour hospital is asking you to take a risk you do not need to take.
</p>
