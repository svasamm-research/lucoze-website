<h2 id="five">What must a clinic do under DPDP?</h2>
<ol>
	<li>
		<strong>Tell patients what data you collect and why.</strong> A printed notice at the front desk
		plus a consent line on intake forms is the floor.
	</li>
	<li>
		<strong>Get explicit consent before sharing data with anyone</strong> — labs, insurance, even
		the doctor's WhatsApp group. “Implied consent” is not a thing under DPDP.
	</li>
	<li>
		<strong>Let patients access, correct, and delete their data on request.</strong> Including old
		paper records you've digitised.
	</li>
	<li>
		<strong>Appoint someone</strong> — anyone, with a name and a phone number — as your Data
		Protection point of contact. The administrator is fine.
	</li>
	<li>
		<strong>Be able to detect breaches.</strong> If patient data leaks, you have 72 hours to notify
		the regulator. Not 72 working days. 72 hours.
	</li>
</ol>

<h2 id="three">What can a clinic ignore for now?</h2>
<ul>
	<li>
		<strong>Significant Data Fiduciary classification.</strong> Applies to platforms, not individual
		clinics. Unless you operate across multiple hospitals at scale, this is not your problem yet.
	</li>
	<li>
		<strong>Cross-border localisation rules.</strong> Currently in flux. If your software vendor
		stores data in India (ask them — see below), you are covered.
	</li>
	<li>
		<strong>DPDP-specific certifications.</strong> None exist as of May 2026. Save your money.
	</li>
</ul>

<h2 id="question">What one question should you ask any vendor?</h2>
<blockquote>Where do you store our patient data, geographically?</blockquote>
<p>
	If the answer is “the US” or “we'll get back to you” — that's a procurement red flag. If the
	answer is “in India, in [named region]” — you're fine.
</p>
