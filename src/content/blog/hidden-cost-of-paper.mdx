<h2 id="afternoon">An afternoon at a 6-doctor clinic</h2>
<p>
	Last week I sat next to Sutapa, who has run the front desk at a 6-doctor clinic in Salt Lake,
	Kolkata, for nine years. Not a sales call. Not a demo. Just four hours, a paper appointment
	register, and a margin to scribble in.
</p>

<h2 id="counts">What I counted</h2>
<ul>
	<li>3 tools open simultaneously, none of them talking. The “system” is Sutapa's memory.</li>
	<li>62 phone calls handled — 30 to reschedule, 18 new bookings, 14 “did the doctor say…”.</li>
	<li>
		42 seconds average to draw a line through one slot and write into another, then open WhatsApp to
		confirm.
	</li>
	<li>Every printed receipt logged a second time by hand into a physical ledger.</li>
	<li>
		Twice during the afternoon, the doctor came out and asked, “Was Mr Banerjee from last Tuesday in
		for diabetes or thyroid?” Sutapa remembered both times.
	</li>
</ul>

<h2 id="math">The math</h2>
<p>
	Conservative version. 42 seconds per rescheduling × 30 reschedules per day = 21 minutes of
	friction. Across a 6-day week, 2 hours. Across a year, 100+ hours of one staff member's time spent
	on a process that should be one tap.
</p>
<p>
	Less conservative version. The cost when Sutapa is on leave and her replacement — hired three
	months ago — takes 4-6× longer for the same operations. The doctor's question goes unanswered. The
	patient who called twice ends up at a competitor.
</p>

<h2 id="lesson">The lesson I keep relearning</h2>
<p>
	The constraint at most Indian clinics is not “lack of software.” It's that the software they
	bought was designed by people who never sat next to Sutapa for 4 hours.
</p>
