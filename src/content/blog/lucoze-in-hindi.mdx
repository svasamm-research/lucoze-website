<h2 id="patna">A morning in Patna</h2>
<p>
	I spent yesterday at a 4-doctor clinic on Boring Road, Patna. They use three pieces of software.
	All three have English-only UIs. The front-desk staff have memorised the menu paths because they
	cannot actually read the labels.
</p>
<p>
	When the receptionist who knew the paths left in February, the clinic spent six weeks training her
	replacement. Six weeks of slower billing, missed appointments, and one near-miss patient ID
	mix-up.
</p>

<h2 id="ask">What helped most</h2>
<p>
	I asked the owner what would have helped most. Not “more features.” Not “better integration.”
	Hindi interface labels. In Devanagari. With Hindi error messages.
</p>

<h2 id="why">Why this is so rarely done</h2>
<p>
	Most clinic SaaS in India was built in Bangalore or Bombay by founders whose own clinical
	familiarity is English-first. The local-language UI gets relegated to a “regional features”
	footnote, often half-translated, often untested with a real receptionist.
</p>
<p>
	The result, in Tier-2 East India: a 6–8 month staff retraining cost baked into the cost of using
	the software.
</p>

<h2 id="us">What we're doing</h2>
<ul>
	<li>Hindi UI ships in Q3 2026 — as a first-class primary UI, not a translation overlay.</li>
	<li>Bengali after that. Odia after that.</li>
	<li>
		All error messages, confirmation prompts, and WhatsApp templates translated by clinicians who
		actually work in that language. No Google Translate shortcuts.
	</li>
	<li>Patient-counsel sheets print in the language the patient reads.</li>
</ul>

<h2 id="competition">The real competition</h2>
<p>You compete with paper in Bihar. Paper is in Hindi. Software has to be too.</p>
