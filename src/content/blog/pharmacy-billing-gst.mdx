<p>
	Most clinic owners know two things about GST and healthcare: that health services are largely
	exempt, and that their pharmacy still charges tax. Those two facts sit uncomfortably next to each
	other, and the pharmacy counter is exactly where the seam shows. A pharmacy invoice has to satisfy
	drug law and tax law at the same time — and the two were written by different people for different
	reasons. This is a practical walk through what belongs on the invoice and where the common
	assumptions go wrong. It is general information, not tax or legal advice; verify specifics with
	your CA and against the current CBIC notifications.
</p>

<h2 id="not-exempt">Why isn't pharmacy exempt if healthcare is?</h2>
<p>
	The exemption most people remember applies to <em>health-care services</em> — a doctor treating a
	patient. A pharmacy selling medicines and consumables over the counter is a <strong>supply of
	goods</strong>, and goods are taxable unless specifically exempt. So an outpatient walking up to
	buy a strip of tablets is a taxable sale, GST and all.
</p>
<p>
	The genuinely tricky case is medicines supplied to an <strong>admitted patient</strong> as part of
	treatment. Several advance rulings have treated in-patient medicines and consumables as part of a
	single <em>composite</em> health-care service, and therefore exempt — but this is fact-specific and
	has been litigated both ways. Don't generalise from a headline. Where your outpatient pharmacy and
	your in-patient supply are billed differently, that difference has a reason, and your CA should
	sign off on it. Our{" "}
	<a href="/in/blog/gst-for-clinics/">GST for clinics and hospitals guide</a> covers the exempt/
	taxable line for the clinic as a whole; this post is about the counter.
</p>

<h2 id="hsn">What is HSN, and what rate do medicines carry now?</h2>
<p>
	<strong>HSN</strong> (Harmonised System of Nomenclature) is the code that tells the tax system what
	a product is. Most pharmaceuticals fall under HSN Chapter 30, but the exact code — and the GST rate
	that rides on it — varies by formulation. A GST-registered seller above the prescribed turnover has
	to show HSN on the invoice, and the number of digits required scales with turnover.
</p>
<p>
	The rates changed materially under <strong>GST 2.0</strong>. On the 56th GST Council's
	recommendation, from <strong>22 September 2025</strong> most drugs and medicines moved from the 12%
	slab to <strong>5%</strong>, while a specified set of life-saving drugs was cut to <strong>Nil</strong>
	(33 drugs from 12% to nil, plus three cancer/rare-disease drugs from 5% to nil). So the working
	picture today: <strong>5% on the great majority of formulations, nil on the notified life-saving
	list</strong>, with non-medicine counter items (cosmetics, some devices, general goods) sitting
	higher on their own HSN.
</p>
<p>
	The practical point survives the rate change: <strong>the rate is HSN-specific, not
	"pharmacy-specific."</strong> If your billing treats "the pharmacy" as one flat rate you'll mis-tax
	part of the shelf — the nil-rated life-savers or the higher-rated non-medicines — and it surfaces at
	return time. Lists and rates move by notification, so confirm the current rate for a given HSN
	against CBIC rather than hard-coding it.
</p>

<h2 id="schedule-h">Where does Schedule H come in — isn't that drug law, not tax?</h2>
<p>
	It is drug law, and it lives on the same invoice, which is why pharmacies conflate the two. Under
	the Drugs and Cosmetics Rules, prescription-only medicines are grouped into schedules:
</p>
<ul>
	<li>
		<strong>Schedule H</strong> — prescription-only drugs. Dispensed against a valid prescription,
		with the sale recorded.
	</li>
	<li>
		<strong>Schedule H1</strong> — a subset (certain antibiotics, some habit-forming drugs) with
		<em>extra</em> record-keeping: a dedicated register capturing the drug, quantity, prescriber, and
		patient, retained for the period the rules require.
	</li>
	<li>
		<strong>Schedule X</strong> — the most tightly controlled (narcotic and psychotropic
		categories), with stricter storage, a separate register, and prescription-retention rules.
	</li>
</ul>
<p>
	None of this changes the GST on the line, but it changes what your system has to <em>capture</em> at
	the point of sale — a prescriber, a patient, a batch, an expiry — and hold for inspection. A counter
	that captures GST but not the H1 register is compliant with the tax man and exposed to the drug
	inspector. Getting both from one entry, rather than a bill on the computer and a register in a
	notebook, is the whole point of running the pharmacy on the same system as the clinic.
</p>

<h2 id="batch-expiry">Why do batch and expiry belong on the bill?</h2>
<p>
	Beyond law, batch and expiry are where pharmacy <em>money</em> leaks. Stock bought and never sold
	before expiry is a straight write-off; stock sold without recording the batch can't be traced in a
	recall. A pharmacy that bills by batch — oldest-expiry-first, expiry visible at the counter —
	converts "we found expired stock during stock-take" into "the system flagged it a month out." That
	is inventory discipline, not heroics, and it is exactly the kind of quiet leak the{" "}
	<a href="/in/features/pharmacy/">pharmacy module</a> is meant to close. Lucoze is built to track
	Schedule-H/H1/X dispensing, batch, and expiry from the same sale that produces the GST invoice, so
	the compliance record and the tax record come from one action instead of two.
</p>

<h2 id="e-invoice">Do we need e-invoicing for the pharmacy?</h2>
<p>
	<strong>E-invoicing</strong> — reporting B2B invoices to the government portal to get an IRN before
	issuing them — is mandatory once a business crosses the aggregate-turnover threshold. Since{" "}
	<strong>1 August 2023</strong> (CBIC Notification 10/2023-Central Tax) that threshold is{" "}
	<strong>₹5 crore</strong>: if your aggregate turnover crossed ₹5 crore in <em>any</em> financial
	year from 2017-18 onward, e-invoicing applies to your B2B supplies and exports. Note "aggregate" —
	it's your total turnover across the whole GSTIN/entity, not the pharmacy in isolation, so a hospital
	with pharmacy, lab, and OPD revenue can cross the line without any single counter looking large.
</p>
<p>
	Two more things worth knowing: businesses with turnover of <strong>₹10 crore or more</strong> must
	report invoices to the IRP within <strong>30 days</strong> of the invoice date (effective 1 April
	2025) — miss the window and the IRN can't be generated. And B2C counter sales are a separate regime
	(dynamic-QR rules) with their own thresholds. If you're near ₹5 crore, that's a question for your CA
	now, not at the next audit — and your billing should be able to switch e-invoicing on when you cross
	the line without re-platforming.
</p>
