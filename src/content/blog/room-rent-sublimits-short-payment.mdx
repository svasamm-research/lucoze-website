<p>
	One of the most confusing moments in hospital billing is the claim that was <em>approved</em> and
	still gets paid short. Nothing was rejected. The pre-auth came through, the patient was treated, the
	documents were clean — and the settlement is thousands less than the bill. Nine times out of ten the
	culprit isn't fraud or error. It's a <strong>room-rent sub-limit</strong> doing exactly what the
	policy said it would, in a way nobody explained to the patient at admission. This is a plain walk
	through the mechanism so it stops surprising your billing desk. It's general information — the exact
	clauses live in each patient's policy.
</p>

<h2 id="sublimit">What is a room-rent sub-limit?</h2>
<p>
	Many health policies cap what they'll pay for the room per day — often as a percentage of the sum
	insured (say, a set percent per day), or a fixed amount, or by room <em>category</em> ("shared" or
	a named tier). Take a room at or below that cap and nothing happens. Take a room <em>above</em> it —
	because it's what was available, or what the family asked for — and you've triggered a clause most
	people have never read.
</p>

<h2 id="proportionate">Why does a bigger room cut more than just the room?</h2>
<p>
	This is the part that catches people. Many policies with a room cap also carry a{" "}
	<strong>proportionate deduction</strong> clause: because higher room categories often come with
	higher linked charges (nursing, some professional fees), exceeding the eligible room rent lets the
	insurer scale down defined "associate medical expenses" in the same proportion — not only the room
	line. A modest room upgrade can therefore shave a percentage off other charges too, which is how
	"approved" quietly becomes "paid short."
</p>
<h2 id="whatchanged">What changed in 2024 — and why it matters at your desk</h2>
<p>
	Crucially, the scope of that deduction is now limited. Under the IRDAI{" "}
	<strong>Health Insurance Master Circular dated 29 May 2024</strong>, room-rent sub-limits remain a
	permitted policy feature <em>but must be clearly disclosed</em>, and proportionate deduction can only
	be applied to the defined set of associate medical expenses. Insurers <strong>cannot</strong> apply
	it to <strong>pharmacy and medicines, implants, medical devices, or diagnostics</strong>. In other
	words, the old practice of scaling down the <em>entire</em> bill against a room overage is no longer
	permissible — the deduction is narrower than many billing desks (and many insurers' older systems)
	still assume.
</p>
<p>
	That cuts both ways for your desk. The genuine shortfall on an above-cap room is smaller than it used
	to be — but that also means a settlement that <em>does</em> deduct from pharmacy, implants, devices,
	or diagnostics on room-rent grounds is one you can question, citing the current circular. You only
	catch it if you reconcile.
</p>

<h2 id="desk">What can the billing desk actually do about it?</h2>
<p>
	You can't rewrite the patient's policy, but you can stop the surprise — and surprise is what turns a
	short payment into a bad debt and an argument at discharge:
</p>
<ul>
	<li>
		<strong>Check the room eligibility at admission, not discharge.</strong> The pre-auth is the
		moment to know the room cap and flag it. A patient who chooses a higher room having been told the
		proportion applies is a very different conversation from one who finds out on the final bill.
	</li>
	<li>
		<strong>Estimate the likely deduction up front.</strong> If the room is above cap, the shortfall
		is roughly predictable. Telling the family "the insurer will cover about this much, and this much
		is yours" at admission is honest and prevents the discharge stand-off.
	</li>
	<li>
		<strong>Reconcile every settlement against the bill.</strong> When payment lands short, someone has
		to check whether the deduction matches the policy's clause or whether the insurer over-deducted —
		which does happen and can be contested. Without that reconciliation step, you eat both the fair
		deductions and the unfair ones.
	</li>
</ul>

<h2 id="tracking">Why this is a tracking problem, not a negotiation one</h2>
<p>
	The hospitals that bleed on sub-limits aren't the ones with tough insurers — they're the ones
	without a running view of what was billed versus what was settled. When every claim carries its
	approved amount, its settled amount, and the gap, a pattern of short payments becomes visible and
	the genuinely wrong ones become contestable. This is the same reconciliation discipline that keeps
	claims from ageing, described in our{" "}
	<a href="/in/blog/tpa-insurance-claims-clinics/">TPA and insurance claims</a> guide. Lucoze is built
	to hold approved-versus-settled on each claim so a short payment is a flagged gap to review, not a
	number that quietly disappears into "insurance is like that."
</p>
